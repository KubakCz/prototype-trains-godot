# Level authoring

How a level is built: snapping rails onto the terrain, welding a branch onto a turnout, the
other way round - a terrain carved to fit the rails - and the playground, a level generated by a
script. What a turnout *is* is in [turnouts.md](turnouts.md).

## Snapping rails to the ground

`Rail` snaps its curve points onto the ground:

- In the editor: the **Snap Points To Surface** inspector button.
- At runtime: `snap_on_ready` (on by default). It deliberately does *not* run automatically in
  the editor, so opening a scene never silently modifies it.
- `surface_root` points at the ground node; unset falls back to the `snap_surface` group.

Snapping only moves the points and their handles, so **point spacing must be finer than the
terrain's features** — the curve between two snapped points can still float over a dip.
On `Ground`'s analytic hills (`hill_height` 2.2), 15 points across 200 m kept the old main scene's
main line within 0.22 m of its `surface_offset`; 5 points left it 1.5 m out in places, and the
three-point branch first drawn for `tests/trackside_posts_test.gd` floated 2 m over a dip.

## Authoring a turnout

1. Draw the branch rail so its start or end lands roughly on the main rail.
2. Add a `Turnout`, point it at both rails, and set `main_distance`, `diverge_towards` and
   `branch_end`. Placement is numeric — gizmo-drag placement belongs to step 8's editor.
3. Press **Align Branch To Turnout**. It welds the branch's attached end onto the points and
   turns its handle so the branch leaves **tangent to the main rail**. Tangency is what makes
   the handover smooth: a train crossing the points must not change direction abruptly, so the
   branch is expected to curve away over its *next* segment, not at the points.
4. Check `_get_configuration_warnings()` is clear — it flags a drifted branch, an impossible
   departure angle, a `main_distance` off the rail, and a toe leg with no length.

`align_branch_on_ready` (on by default, runtime only) re-runs step 3 when the level starts.
Surface snapping moves each rail onto the terrain independently, so without it a hand-aligned
branch drifts off the points and trains visibly jump on handover.

There is no editor button available to a headless agent. To weld a branch without the editor:
instantiate the scene, set `snap_on_ready = false` on the rails **before** adding it to the
tree, call `align_branch_to_turnout()`, then print the curve's `_data` and paste it back into
the `.tscn`.

`Rail`'s debug draw shows both running rails, sleepers, **cyan chevrons for the curve's own
direction** (trains may run against them), and a green start / red end marker. Rail direction
is level data, not cosmetic — turnouts are defined relative to it. Since step 6 the real track
is a generated mesh and the line drawing is **off by default**: switch it on per rail
(`debug_draw`), or for every rail at once with the turnout debug panel's **rail lines** row.


Snapping only moves curve points and handles, so point spacing has to be finer than the
terrain's features. If real levels get hillier, per-segment subdivision may be worth adding.

## The track, bridges and tunnels

Every level needs one `TrackMesher` node (`Track` in every scene here) for its rails to be drawn
at all: it builds the track from the rails, in the editor and as the level loads, and rebuilds
in the editor a moment after a curve stops moving. Nothing it builds is saved. Bridges and
tunnels are nodes too - `Bridge` and `Tunnel`, under `Structures` by convention - each a rail and
a span along it, plus a style for a bridge; the mesher's **Find Bridges & Tunnels** button
proposes them from the terrain. How all of it works is in [track.md](track.md).

A level carved for its tunnels puts each mouth where the carved ground starts to rise, and the
`Tunnel` there: the ground cuts itself open behind the portal for the bore's shell and levels
the apron in front of it. Without a heightmap carved to match, a tunnel's portal stands against
whatever the terrain happens to be. Wherever the ground is left a little high under ordinary
track, the track cuts it down itself (see [track.md](track.md)) - but a cutting's sides are the
carving's business.

## Levels on a baked terrain

Snapping puts rails on whatever ground there is, which is fine on gentle hills and useless for a
tunnel or a bridge: a snapped rail runs over the ridge, and down into the gully. So a level with
either is built the other way round. The rails carry their own heights (`snap_on_ready = false`
on every one of them), and the ground is carved to fit them and baked into a heightmap.

`Ground.heightmap` is an `Image` in `FORMAT_RF`, one height in metres per grid vertex, spread
over `Ground.size`, first row at -Z. With it set, `subdivisions` and `hill_height` are ignored
and the image's resolution is the grid. `surface_root` still points at the ground, because the
turnout markers and signal masts stand on it through `Rail.ground_frame()`.

Carving follows three rules, and `tests/desert_level_test.gd` checks the result from the rails'
side:

- **Ordinary track** gets a flat bed 0.25 m (`surface_offset`) under the railhead, 3 m either
  side of the centre line, blending out into cuttings at 1:1 and banks at 1:1.6.
- **A tunnel** is any stretch where the natural ground stands more than 7 m over the railhead,
  grown from a rail's end (a way off the level, at most 90 m) or forced (under a hill). The ground
  over it is kept at least 6.5 m above the railhead. A heightfield can only rise from the bed to
  the roof across one grid cell, so every tunnel mouth is a few metres of ground in the loading
  gauge - the one place that is allowed. Behind each mouth the ground has to stand clear *above*
  the bore's shell (whose roof is 6.5 m up, `Tunnel.SHELL_TOP`), or the shell shows as a stone
  slab sticking out of the hillside: the desert's ground is held 8.5 m over the railhead for the
  first 18 m in, 6.5 m either side of the centre line (the portal's width), falling 1:1 beyond
  that and never into a cutting of other track. The original carving ramped up over the first
  8 m inside the tunnel instead, and every mouth showed its shell; this was added to the baked
  heightmap afterwards.
- **Water** is a flat surface at the water level; the ground makes the shoreline. Through the
  waterline the ground has to be **one straight slope for a couple of grid cells either way**
  (the desert lake: 0.15, a 4 m deep bowl inside, blending back into the terrain 6-16 m out).
  A kink near the waterline - a flat beach meeting a steep drop - is interpolated differently
  by each grid triangle, and the shore comes out as a staircase. Nothing below the water level
  may open onto the shore either: the dry wash north of the desert lake used to, and showed as
  a notch. It is now the lake's outflow, `River`: a ribbon of water (`desert_river.res`)
  falling 1.5 cm a metre from the lake's level, under `GullyBridge` (4 m below its rails) to
  the north edge of the map, in a channel 16 m wide at the waterline with banks at 0.3 through
  it. The ribbon is 3 m wider each side than the channel, so its edges are buried in the banks.
  The lake's bowl and the river's channel were both reshaped in the baked heightmap after the
  original carving.
- **A bridge** is left alone: no bank is filled under it, or within 30 m of its ends.

Two tracks side by side at different heights cannot both be carved for: a 4 m grid cell spans
the gap between them and interpolates one into the other's loading gauge. Where one line climbs
beside another, both climb.

### The desert level

`scenes/levels/desert.tscn` is a whole level rebuilt from a reference layout, track and terrain
only - no buildings. Its terrain is a baked heightmap on a 3 m grid; the
carver that made it was a one-off and is not in the repo, so moving track means editing the
heightmap to match. Parallel tracks are 5 m apart, the steepest gradient is 5.2% (the north line
beside the ridge) and the tightest radius 28 m (on the loop).

| rail | from - to |
| --- | --- |
| `BottomLine` | the west exit to the east exit, straight along the south edge |
| `NorthLine` | the north-east exit west to `BridgeJunction`, straight along the north edge |
| `LoopLine` | `SouthJunction`, up through the wye, over the ridge, along the north line, over the bridge, round the lake, through the tunnel, back to `TunnelJunction` |
| `EastLine` | `WyeNorth`, round to the east exit |
| `WyeChord` | `WyeEast` to `WyeSouth`, the triangle's third side |
| `ScissorsDown` / `ScissorsUp` | the two diagonals of `LakeScissors` |

The loop round the lake is a **balloon**: out over the bridge, back through the tunnel, onto the
north line facing the other way. It is one rail from `SouthJunction` to `TunnelJunction` because
two rails only ever meet at a turnout; the alternative, the north line looping back onto itself,
works but draws a configuration warning. Where the loop touches the bottom line south of the
lake, `LakeScissors` (a double crossover, `LakeScissorsMiddle` its crossing) joins them; the
reference draws it as an X between two tracks a few pixels apart. The ridge line crosses the
tunnel line 8 m above it - the same rail twice, 1050 m apart along it.

Its structures were proposed by **Find Bridges & Tunnels** and written into the scene by hand:
`GullyBridge`, a steel truss on `LoopLine`; `RidgeTunnel`, the loop's tunnel under the ridge; and
a tunnel on each of the four exits (`WestExitTunnel`, `EastExitTunnel`, `NorthExitTunnel`,
`EastLineExitTunnel`), which run off the rails' ends and so have one portal each and swallow the
dead ends.

Every junction is protected from every way into it, sixteen signals in all, and three trains come
out of the exit tunnels at the start and wait at the entry signals: `WestArrival` (two coaches)
at `WestEntry`, `NorthArrival` (three full freight wagons) at `NorthEntry`, `EastArrival` (one
coach) at `EastEntry`. They start from rest a train's length inside each rail's end - a train's
configuration warnings say when its tail hangs off one. The exits are plain dead ends, so a
train sent into one turns round in the dark and comes back (`end_behavior`); see
[further_development.md](further_development.md).

## The playground

`scenes/main.tscn` is the level to test things on: every kind of junction the prototype has, on
a layout small enough that a train gets from one to the next in seconds. It is **generated** by
[`tools/build_playground.gd`](../tools/build_playground.gd):

```bash
godot --headless --path . --script res://tools/build_playground.gd
# Windows: Godot_v4.7.2-stable_win64_console.exe in place of godot
```

The script is the source, not the scene. It draws every rail as a turtle walk of straights and
circular arcs, so each branch leaves its points exactly tangent. It also places the junctions,
the signals and the trains, carves the terrain, and writes `main.tscn` - hand-readable, like the
demos - and `scenes/main_heights.res`. A re-run overwrites both, so an edit made in the editor
is lost unless it is made in the script too. It prints a clearance report: any two stretches of
track closer than 4.6 m in plan away from a junction.

Everything runs at one height, 0.25 m railhead on flat ground at 0, except the corner beyond the
wye. The layout, with the oval centred 110 m west of the origin so that the whole level is
centred on the ground:

| rail | from - to |
| --- | --- |
| `NorthLine` | the wye's apex, down the north leg, west along the outer north straight, round the balloon loop, back to `BalloonWest` |
| `OuterLine` | `BalloonEast`, west round the oval's west end, east along the outer south straight, round the single-track east curve to `WyeNorth` |
| `InnerLine` | `SouthMerge`, west along the inner south straight, round the west end, east along the inner north straight to `NorthMerge` |
| `HillLine` | the dead end beyond the hill, west through the tunnel and over the bridge to the apex, down the south leg to `WyeSouth` |
| `CrossoverLink`, `ScissorsDown`, `ScissorsUp` | `NorthCrossover` on the north straight; `SouthScissors` on the south one |
| `FreightLine` | a dead end south of the oval, north over both south tracks on `OuterDiamond` and `InnerDiamond`, east through `YardSlip` to a goods stub |
| `YardLead` | `YardEntry` on the inner north straight, through `YardSlip` and `YardThreeWay` to a dead end |
| `YardLeft`, `YardRight`, `SlipRoadNorth`, `SlipRoadSouth` | the three-way's side roads and the slip's two slip roads |

**Signals protect every way into every junction**, on both tracks in both directions: 46 in all.
A signal facing the points stands 8 m short of them; one a train meets trailing in stands 30 m
out, clear of the other leg; one before a crossing, 10 m. The two diamonds are 5 m apart and are
protected as one, so the freight line's pair serves both. `main_scene_test.gd` walks out from
every junction along every leg and checks that the first thing on the rail is a signal governing
the way back in. **Four trains** open the level running up to a red, one in each part of it: `Express`
(a locomotive and a coach) on the outer north straight, `Freight` (three full wagons) off the
bridge, `Regional` on the freight line south of the diamonds, `Shunter` (two empty wagons) in the
yard. The generator places each by its nose, a few metres short of its signal, so the rest of
the consist goes behind it however long it is - and asserts it fits on its rail.

**The carved corner** is a gully the tail crosses on a 21 m bridge, a stream in its bottom, and
a hill it goes through in a 44 m tunnel. The generator records each stretch it carved as a bridge
or a tunnel and writes a `Bridge` (stone arches) and a `Tunnel` for them, under `Structures`. There is also a shallow pond inside the balloon loop,
with no track near it. The carving follows the desert's three rules, with one change: each grid
vertex takes its orders from the *nearest* point of each rail, not from every point in reach.
Otherwise the 1:1 cutting up to a tunnel mouth eats its slopes several metres into the hill, and
the mouth leaves the loading gauge for more than one grid cell.

### Every loop has to be cut at a junction

Two rails only ever meet at a turnout, and there they meet one way: one rail runs through the
points, the other's *end* is welded on. There is no end-to-end join, and a rail welded to
itself draws a warning. So a closed loop of track cannot be one rail, and it cannot be closed by
butting two rails together either. It has to be cut at junctions where the loop takes the
*diverging* route and the rail through the points carries on somewhere else.

The playground's outer track closes like this. `NorthLine` comes off the wye's apex as a branch,
runs through `WyeNorth` and along the north straight, through `BalloonEast` *into the loop*, and
ends welded at `BalloonWest`. `OuterLine` starts welded at `BalloonEast`, runs through
`BalloonWest` and on round the rest of the oval, and ends welded at `WyeNorth`. Every rail end is
on a different rail, and each turnout's toe still faces the way a train has to choose. Read the
other way round, that rules out the obvious layouts. With only the wye, the oval cannot be
closed: every choice of through routes at its three turnouts welds some rail to itself. The
balloon's two junctions are what make it work.

The inner track avoids the problem by not being a loop of its own. It leaves the outer track at
`SouthMerge` and rejoins it at `NorthMerge`, both ends welded, so the east curve - where the wye
leaves - is single track. A full inner oval would need two more junctions on it with somewhere
to go. A pair of stub sidings would do, each the through route at its own points, facing away
from each other.
